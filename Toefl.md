
As of 2026, the TOEFL iBT has changed from the older 0–120-style framing, so use the current ETS scoring and test format when you book your exam. For preparation, the core skills remain Reading, Listening, Speaking, and Writing.

🎯 Target

For MS CSE abroad, I’d aim for:

A score comfortably above your universities’ minimum requirements, rather than simply “passing.”

If your target universities accept/require a high English score, aim for the equivalent of strong C1-level performance.

⸻

🧠 The winning TOEFL formula

Diagnostic → skill building → question patterns → timed practice → mock tests → error elimination

You don’t need 6 months.

For someone with reasonably good English:

4–8 weeks is usually enough

with consistent practice.

⸻

📚 1. READING

The goal isn’t to understand every word.

It’s:

Understand the structure and retrieve information quickly.

When reading an academic passage, identify:

* Main idea
* Purpose
* Important details
* Cause/effect
* Contrast
* Examples
* Author’s reasoning

Don’t translate everything into Hindi.

That wastes time.

Train yourself to understand directly in English.

⸻

🔥 Vocabulary

Don’t try to memorise 10,000 words.

Learn words from:

* TOEFL passages
* Academic vocabulary
* Your mistakes

For every unfamiliar word:

word → meaning → synonym → sentence → recall

Example:

mitigate
= make something less severe
synonym: alleviate
opposite: aggravate

Then use it in a sentence.

⸻

📖 Reading practice

Every day:

30–45 minutes

Read academic English from areas such as:

* Science
* History
* Psychology
* Biology
* Technology

Then ask:

What was the author’s main point?

Why was this example included?

What changed from paragraph 2 to paragraph 3?

This develops TOEFL-style comprehension.

⸻

🎧 2. LISTENING

This is one of the easiest sections to improve systematically.

Listen to academic English regularly.

Train for:

Main idea

Supporting details

Speaker’s attitude

Changes/contrasts

Cause-effect

Examples

Organisation

⸻

📝 Note-taking

Don’t attempt to write every sentence.

Use:

Main idea

↓

Point 1

→ example

↓

Point 2

→ example

↓

Conclusion/attitude

Your notes should help you reconstruct the lecture.

⸻

🗣️ 3. SPEAKING

This is where many technically strong students lose marks.

You don’t need a fake American/British accent.

You need:

Clear + organised + fluent + understandable English.

⸻

🔥 Speaking structure

Train yourself to answer using:

Point

↓

Reason

↓

Example

↓

Conclusion

For integrated tasks:

Reading/Listening information

↓

Relationship

↓

Explanation

⸻

⏱️ Practice with a timer

This is critical.

Don’t practise speaking by casually talking for 5 minutes.

Use the actual time constraints of the current TOEFL format.

Record yourself.

Then check:

Did I pause too much?

Did I repeat words?

Did I actually answer the question?

Did I give supporting details?

Was my pronunciation understandable?

⸻

🎤 Your biggest speaking enemy

Not grammar.

It’s:

Thinking about grammar while speaking.

You need automatic English production.

For example, instead of mentally constructing:

“I think that perhaps this particular situation…”

train:

“I agree for two reasons.”

Then continue.

⸻

✍️ 4. WRITING

For MS applications, academic writing is particularly relevant.

You need:

Clear thesis/position

Logical organisation

Evidence/examples

Cohesion

Grammar

Vocabulary

Don’t try to sound unnecessarily sophisticated.

This:

“This phenomenon is extremely important because…”

is often better than trying to force complicated vocabulary everywhere.

⸻

🧠 Build a reusable structure

For an academic response:

Introduction

Position + main reasons

Body

Reason 1 → explanation → example

Body

Reason 2 → explanation → example

Conclusion

Restate position

For integrated writing:

Reading claim → lecture response → evidence

Don’t merely copy the source.

Show the relationship between them.

⸻

📅 6-WEEK TOEFL PLAN

Week 1 — Diagnostic

Take a full official-style practice test.

Record:

Reading

Listening

Speaking

Writing

Then identify your weakest section.

⸻

Week 2

Reading

Daily passages

Listening

Daily lectures/conversations

Speaking

2–3 timed responses/day

Writing

3 responses/week

⸻

Week 3

Start timed practice.

Don’t wait until the last week.

⸻

Week 4

Increase difficulty.

Focus heavily on mistakes.

⸻

Week 5

Full mock tests

Approximately:

2 per week

Analyse each one carefully.

⸻

Week 6

No massive new material.

Focus on:

Weak areas + timing + vocabulary + speaking fluency + writing quality

⸻

📊 Make an error log

For Reading:

* Vocabulary
* Inference
* Detail
* Main idea
* Time

Listening:

* Missed detail
* Notes
* Inference
* Speaker attitude

Speaking:

* Fluency
* Pronunciation
* Organisation
* Grammar
* Vocabulary

Writing:

* Organisation
* Grammar
* Development
* Vocabulary
* Cohesion

Then attack the biggest category.

⸻

⏰ Daily schedule

For a serious 6-week preparation:

2.5–3 hours/day

30 min — Reading

30 min — Listening

30–45 min — Speaking

30–45 min — Writing

15–20 min — Vocabulary/error review

You can reduce this if your baseline score is already strong.

⸻

🏆 The most important resource rule

Use official ETS material first.

Third-party material is useful for additional practice, but your understanding of the real exam should come from current official TOEFL material.

Don’t prepare using an old TOEFL format without checking that it matches the current exam.

⸻

🔥 If your English is already good

Suppose you’re comfortable watching English lectures, reading technical papers, and communicating in English.

Then don’t spend months studying grammar.

Your preparation should become:

Exam format → timed practice → speaking → writing → error elimination

You could potentially become test-ready in 3–6 weeks.

⸻

🚨 If your English foundation is weak

Then don’t rush directly into TOEFL mocks.

Spend the first few weeks improving:

* Vocabulary
* Sentence construction
* Listening comprehension
* Speaking fluency
* Academic reading

Then transition into TOEFL-specific practice.

⸻

🎯 For an MS CSE applicant

I’d prioritise the sections like this:

Reading + Listening

→ Build academic comprehension

Speaking

→ Build fluency and structured responses

Writing

→ Build concise academic communication

And simultaneously improve your real academic English, because you’ll need it after admission for:

* Lectures
* Research papers
* Presentations
* TA/RA communication
* Interviews
* SOP/research writing

⸻

The golden rule

Don’t ask:

“How many TOEFL questions did I solve?”

Ask:

“Why did I lose those marks, and have I eliminated that problem?”

That’s how you move from an average score to a very strong TOEFL score.
